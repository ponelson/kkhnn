Roth vs. Traditional
two paths, one princess

Both IRAs share a single combined $7,500 limit in 2026 ($8,600 if you're 50+). You may split between them, but the total can't exceed the cap. Choose based on whether your tax bracket today is lower (Roth wins) or higher (Traditional wins) than your expected bracket in retirement.

Backdoor Roth: If income exceeds the Roth cap, contribute to a Traditional IRA (no income limit), then convert to Roth. The conversion is taxable, but if the Traditional contribution was non-deductible, the tax is minimal. Standard move once income climbs above $168K single.
529 Plan: No income limits, no federal contribution cap. Some states give a state-tax deduction (typically $5K–$10K/yr). Tax-free for tuition, fees, books, room & board, plus up to $10K/yr K–12 tuition. SECURE 2.0 lets you roll up to $35K lifetime from a 529 into a Roth IRA after the account has been open 15 years — a powerful escape hatch if college isn't needed.
SEP IRA: The queen of self-employment accounts. The moment you earn 1099 income — freelance work, tutoring, an Etsy shop, thy own LLC — a SEP IRA lets you stash up to 25% of net self-employment earnings, capped at roughly $72,000 in 2026. That's nearly ten times the Roth limit. Funded pre-tax like a Traditional IRA: deduct now, grow tax-deferred, pay ordinary income tax on withdrawal. No income cap to contribute and no Roth phase-out to worry about. The employer (which is you, when self-employed) makes the contributions; the employee does not. RMDs begin at 73. Best wielded later in life when 1099 income flows — but worth knowing now so the door is open the moment thou earnest a freelance dollar.
What Coin May Enter
the rules of royal contribution

Not all gold may pass into thy retirement vault. IRAs — Roth, Traditional, and SEP — demand earned income. That means wages from a W-2 job, 1099 freelance pay, tips, commissions, or net self-employment profit, all reported to the IRS. Allowance, gifts, inheritance, investment income, and most scholarships do not count. The annual contribution is capped at the lesser of the legal limit ($7,500 for 2026) or thy total earned income for that year.

The Math of Limits: Thy max IRA contribution is the lesser of the cap or thy earned income. Earn $2,000 babysitting → max $2,000. Earn $9,000 at a summer job → max $7,500 (the 2026 cap). Earn $0 → max $0. Contributing more triggers a 6% excise tax per year until removed.
The Royal Loophole: The earnings must be thine, but the dollars contributed need not be. If Karina earns $2,000 tutoring, a grandparent may gift her $2,000 to fund the Roth — she keeps her own paycheck for spending, and the contribution is still valid up to her earned amount. Document everything: a simple ledger of dates, hours, work performed, and who paid — and file the income on a tax return (even if no tax is owed) — creates the paper trail the IRS would expect.
Family Hires: A parent who owns a sole proprietorship or single-member LLC may pay a child for legitimate work (modeling for the website, filing, errands) at a reasonable wage. The child's earnings up to the standard deduction (~$15K in 2026) are federal-income-tax-free, and under age 18 they're also exempt from Social Security and Medicare tax. Those wages then become eligible Roth fuel — a favorite move of small-business families.
Custodial vs. Adult: Until age 18 (or 21 in a few states), a minor's Roth IRA must be a custodial Roth IRA, held in a parent's name on behalf of the child. The earned-income rule still applies — the child must have earnings of her own. Brokerages like Fidelity, Schwab, and Vanguard open these for free. Control transfers to the child at the age of majority.
